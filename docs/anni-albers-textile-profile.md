# Anni Albers — Textile Practice Profile

How Anni Albers (1899–1994) built textiles: materials, looms, structures, and the ideas that drove the work. Compiled for Albers Studio from museum guides, her own writing, and scholarship.

---

## Snapshot

Anni Albers treated weaving as a **structural language**, not as illustration painted in yarn. Colour, texture, and pattern came from how warp and weft interlace — and from what the yarn itself could do — rather than from copying a cartoon designed by a painter. She worked across three parallel tracks:

1. **Independent artworks** (“pictorial weavings”)
2. **Architectural / interior textiles** (drapery, room dividers, prototypes for industry)
3. **Teaching and theory** (Black Mountain College; *On Designing*, 1959; *On Weaving*, 1965)

A useful short definition in her words: pictorial weaving is “a form of weaving that is pictorial in character, in contrast to pattern weaving, which deals with repeats of contrasting areas” — art made *with* weave processes, not decoration applied *on top of* them.

## Timeline of practice

| Period | Context | What she built |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| 1922–1933 | Bauhaus (Weimar → Dessau → Berlin) | Wall hangings; industrial prototypes; diploma sound-absorbing wall cloth |
| 1933–1949 | Black Mountain College (NC) | Teaching studio; early pictorial weavings; material experiments |
| 1949–1960s | New Haven, CT | Mature pictorial weavings; architectural commissions; MoMA solo show (1949) |
| Mid-1960s onward | After leaving the loom | Printmaking that continues textile thinking (grid, knot, line, texture) |

She entered the Bauhaus weaving workshop reluctantly (“the least objectionable choice”) and stayed because “threads caught my imagination.” Despite Bauhaus equality ideals, weaving was quickly typed as the “Women’s Workshop”; Albers and peers (notably under Gunta Stölzl) still built a rigorous modernist textile language from the grid and from touch.

## How a piece was built (process)

Albers’ studio process, reconstructed from Tate’s loom documentation and her theory, looks roughly like this:

1. **Idea from structure, not from picture**  
   Start from what warp/weft and a chosen yarn can do — contrast of matte/shiny, thick/thin, open/closed — rather than translating a painting into tapestry.

2. **Threading plan / draft**  
   Plan how warps are threaded through heddles and which shafts lift. Draft notation (diagrams of threading + treadling + tie-up) is the “score” of the cloth. Albers later published and taught these notations extensively in *On Weaving*.

3. **Warping**  
   Measure and order warp ends (often on a warping mill), keep the cross so sequence cannot scramble, wind onto the loom’s back beam with paper between layers.

4. **Dressing the loom**  
   Space warps with a raddle; thread heddles; sley the reed; tie onto the front. For complex pieces this is slow, patient, hand–eye work — Albers valued that slowness as a condition for experiment.

5. **Weaving**  
   Open a shed (lift selected warps), pass the weft shuttle, beat with the reed, advance the warp. Repeat. Pattern and image emerge from the lift sequence and from which yarns are introduced where.

6. **Sample archive**  
   She kept hundreds of labelled samples — miniature tests of yarn, weave, and colour that functioned as both sketches and memory. Samples were as important as finished hangings.

She worked on handlooms (including an eight-harness Structo Artcraft for many pictorial weavings) and also designed for **machine / Jacquard** production when the structure demanded it (e.g. ambitious Bauhaus wall hangings analyzed as requiring many shafts).

## Structures she relied on

### Fundamentals (always present)

From *On Weaving*: nearly all cloth elaborates **plain**, **twill**, or **satin**. Plain weave is the oldest and clearest expression of right-angle interlacing — equal warp and weft presence, firm, rectangular, reversible in character.

### Double (and multi-layer) weave

Used for depth and colour interchange: two (or more) independent layers woven together, locking at edges or where design swaps face and back. Harvard’s analysis of the 1926 *Wall Hanging* describes a **double-weave** with glossy silk warps against matte wefts — black fields enriched by a gold under-layer, analogous to underpainting. Multi-layer cloth was also her bridge to ancient Peruvian practice (double, triple, even rare quadruple weaves), which she held as the richest textile culture she knew.

### Leno / gauze

Warp ends twist around each other as they enclose the weft, opening the plane and making structure visible. Common in 1950s pictorial weavings (e.g. works discussed by Tate; *Red and Blue Layers*, 1954, cited in secondary literature).

### Floating weft & brocade

Extra wefts ride on the surface of a ground weave so she could **draw with thread** — free lines inside the cloth rather than embroidery applied afterward. Tate highlights floating weft and brocade as how she integrated line into structure.

### Open vs packed; tactility as composition

She wrote that if sculpture deals with volume and painting with colour, weaving deals primarily with **tactile effects**: rough/smooth, dull/shiny, hard/soft, plus colour, with texture dominant. Architectural fabrics (e.g. Rockefeller Guest House drapery) pair chenille, cellophane, and metallic/Lurex so structure and material *are* the image — plain weave used to show the yarns, not to hide them under pictorial tapestry technique.

## Materials

Albers mixed **ancient** and **industrial** fibres without hierarchy:

- Silk (gloss vs matte contrast in Bauhaus hangings)
- Cotton, linen, and other naturals
- Chenille (soft bulk)
- Cellophane / synthetics
- Metallic and plastic-coated metal yarns (light-reflecting interiors)

Teaching at Black Mountain pushed this further: students studied paper, grass, metal shavings, corn kernels — “You learn what the material tells you and what the technique tells you.” Backstrap looms from Latin American travel were used pedagogically to reconnect body, tension, and grid.

## Two product lines, one mind

| | Pictorial weavings | Useful / architectural textiles |
|--|-------------------|----------------------------------|
| Purpose | To be looked at — “not to be sat on, walked on” | Drapery, wall coverings, room dividers, industrial prototypes |
| Scale | Often smaller handloom pieces (1950s New Haven) | Yardage concepts; commissions with Gropius, Rockefeller Guest House, etc. |
| Logic | Non-repeating “picture” built in structure | Repeat-capable designs for manufacture; haptic performance (sound absorption, light reflection, spatial division) |

The 1949 MoMA solo exhibition — first for a textile artist there — staged all three: free-hanging dividers, fabric by the yard, and pictorial weavings.

Major late commission: **Six Prayers** (1966–67), six panels for the Jewish Museum as a Holocaust memorial — threads used “as a sculptor or painter uses his medium to produce a scriptural effect.”

## Ancient Peru as teacher

Albers dedicated *On Weaving* to “her great teachers, the weavers of ancient Peru.” She argued their textiles were a full communicative system in a culture without alphabetic writing — and that they possessed essentially every technique known later, plus some unique ones. Museum study in Berlin, then travel and collecting in Mexico, Peru, Cuba, Chile, fed works such as *Ancient Writing* and a lifelong interest in textile-as-code (later titles: *Haiku*, *Code*, *Epitaph*).

This is not nostalgia: she used ancient structures to **revitalise modern practice**, the same way Bauhaus modernism looked to vernacular and pre-industrial craft to go forward.

## Design philosophy (compressed)

- **Material leads.** Listen to yarn and structure; do not dominate them.
- **Grid is generative.** The right-angle warp/weft intersection is the formative law of weaving; stress it rather than disguise it.
- **Handwork prepares industry.** Hand weaving as research and prototype for the power loom — not only as hobby revival (*The Weaver*, 1941 debates).
- **Architecture partnership.** “The Pliable Plane”: textiles as spatial thinking — transparency, opacity, reflection, subdivision — not afterthought upholstery.
- **Tactile sensibility.** Modern life dulls touch; weaving re-educates it.
- **Slowness as method.** Dressing and weaving a handloom create the time in which experiment happens (Tate: “Slowness was a virtue…”).

## Relevance to Albers Studio

This project’s pipeline echoes her separation of concerns:

| Albers | Studio |
|--------|--------|
| Draft / lift plan | Binary draft from yarn roles → structures |
| Yarn choice & tactility | Palette + weave aesthetic modes (ribbon, cord, handloom) |
| Open vs packed sett | Tightness (inter-yarn gap) |
| Hand irregularity of the loom | Roughness (slide, thickness jitter, tension) |
| Pictorial vs useful cloth | Photo/generative art weaves vs exportable draft for loom-like use |
| Sample archive | Saved profiles / fingerprints |

When tuning **Handloom** mode and **tightness**, you are approximating the same dial she cared about: how much the cloth confesses its making — packed industrial clarity versus open, tactile, slightly irregular handwoven character.

## Short glossary

| Term | Meaning in Albers’ world |
|------|---------------------------|
| **Warp** | Lengthwise yarns under tension on the loom |
| **Weft** | Crosswise yarn carried by the shuttle through the shed |
| **Shed** | Opening between raised and lowered warps |
| **Draft** | Notation of threading, tie-up, and treadling |
| **Sett** | Spacing of warps (and effective density of cloth) |
| **Pictorial weaving** | Non-repeating artwork in woven structure |
| **Leno / gauze** | Twisting warps around weft for open cloth |
| **Double weave** | Two layers woven together; colour/face can interchange |
| **Floating weft** | Extra weft that skips over warps to draw surface lines |
